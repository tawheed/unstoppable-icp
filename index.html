Part 1

What is an Ideal Customer Profile (ICP)?

An Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) is an account-level description of the company most likely to become your best customer. Unlike a Buyer Persona (which describes individuals), an ICP defines organizational characteristics including firmographics, buying triggers, and market trends that indicate a company will get maximum value from your product. B2B SaaS companies with a defined ICP have a 68% higher win rate according to SiriusDecisions research.

Key Takeaway

"An ICP is not a Buyer Persona. It is an account-based definition of the ideal company that gets maximum value from your product. A basic Ideal Customer Profile starts with an exercise that identifies three layers: Firmographics (Size/Location), Triggers (Events like Funding), and Macro Trends (Why buy now?)."

Most Founders and GTM Leaders confuse their TAM (Total Addressable Market) with their ICP. Your TAM is everyone you could sell to. Your ICP is who you should sell to right now. This distinction is critical because trying to sell to your entire TAM immediately results in diluted messaging and wasted AD spend.

The biggest mistake in early-stage B2B sales is creating a "Buyer Persona" (e.g., "Marketing Mary") without first defining the organization she works for. A "Buyer Persona" tells you who to talk to, but an ICP tells you where to find them and when to reach out. Without the ICP, you are just cold calling people who have no organizational budget or urgency.

The 3 Core Components of the Framework:

  • 1
    Firmographics (The "Who") This is the static data. It includes revenue, headcount, geography, and technology stack. For example, "B2B SaaS companies with $10M-$50M ARR running on Salesforce." This filters out the noise.
  • 2
    Triggers (The "When") Triggers are the "proxy for urgency." A company might fit your firmographics but have no need for your product. A trigger event - like hiring a new VP of Sales, raising a Series B round, or missing a quarterly earnings report - signals chaos. Where there is chaos, there is budget.
  • 3
    Macro Trends (The "Why Now") What is shifting in the market that makes your solution inevitable? For example, the shift from "Growth at all costs" to "Profitability" is a macro trend that forces companies to buy efficiency tools. Aligning your ICP with a macro trend creates a "Why Now" narrative that accelerates sales cycles.

Why Define Your ICP?

Without a clearly defined ICP, your sales team chases unqualified leads, your marketing speaks to everyone (and resonates with no one), and your product roadmap gets pulled in ten different directions by customers who shouldn't be customers. An ICP acts as a filter for every decision: which prospects to pursue, which features to build, which markets to enter. When everyone in your organization—from sales reps to engineers—knows exactly who your ideal customer is, you stop wasting resources on accounts that will churn, demand endless customization, or never close in the first place.

Part 3

How Do You Size Your Market with TAM SAM SOM?

As you think about defining your Ideal Customer Profile, it's critical to understand how it fits within your overall market opportunity. That's where TAM, SAM, and SOM come in. TAM (Total Addressable Market) is everyone who could theoretically buy your product. SAM (Serviceable Available Market) is the segment you can actually serve based on your product and geography. SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market) is what you can realistically capture in the near term. Here's the connection most founders miss: your ICP maps directly to your SAM—it's the specific segment you're carving out—and your Initial Customer Profile (the subset that gets you to your next milestone) maps to your SOM. Use our TAM SAM SOM Calculator to size your market opportunity.

Key Takeaway

"Your ICP doesn't exist in a vacuum—it lives within your market sizing framework. Start with your TAM: an existing market where money is already being spent. Your SAM is the segment you carve out based on your product and reach—this is where your ICP lives. Your SOM is your realistic near-term capture—this is your Initial Customer Profile, the accounts that get you to your next revenue milestone. Understanding all three helps you focus your resources and build a realistic path to growth."

Part 4

What Are the 3 Common ICP Mistakes That Kill SaaS Growth?

The three most common ICP mistakes are: (1) relying on intuition instead of data, (2) making your ICP too broad due to fear of missing out, and (3) treating it as a static document instead of a living mandate. Most founders fail at ICP because they write it once and never update it. Your ICP should be reviewed quarterly based on Closed-Won and Closed-Lost data, and revised whenever you launch major features or enter new markets.

Key Takeaway

"Static ICPs fail. An 'Unstoppable ICP' is a living mandate. Mistake #1 is relying on intuition instead of data. Mistake #2 is making it too broad (fear of missing out). Mistake #3 is 'Setting and Forgetting' it."

Why do so many Founders struggle to find Product-Market Fit even after defining an ICP? Because they treat the Ideal Customer Profile as a "One-and-Done" exercise. They write it down in a Google Doc, share it with the team once, and then never look at it again.

However, the market is dynamic. Your product is evolving. If your ICP is static, you are essentially driving with a map from 1999. The most successful founders treat their ICP as an iterative loop—constantly refining it based on new data from sales calls and closed-won deals.

The "FOMO" Trap: The most common mistake is the "Fear Of Missing Out." Founders define a broad ICP because they are afraid that if they niche down, the market will be too small. The irony is that by trying to appeal to everyone, you appeal to no one. You must start with a "Beachhead" - a small, defensible segment where you are 10x better - and expand from there.

Part 6

How Do You Launch a New SaaS Product?

You do not launch with one big bang. You run a multi-threaded launch: two threads running in parallel from the first commit, one building the product and one running go-to-market. It progresses through three principles:

  • 1. Idea Stage. Validate your Who, the urgent problem, and your value proposition against your own network.
  • 2. Consistency. Flesh out your Ideal Customer Profile, your messaging Manifesto, and your first Broadway Show.
  • 3. Scale. Run a dual flank strategy at the whole market.

Slack, Snowflake, and ChatGPT did not big bang launch. They did this.

Most founders treat a launch as a moment. A giant button you press once the product is finally ready, after which the money arrives. Ask a founder who is deep in the build when they are getting in front of real users and the fear shows up immediately. Is the product good enough? Do we have the right features? Is the MVP done? If I show it to people, will someone steal the idea?

That fear postpones the launch, and the postponement is the damage. Launch day eventually arrives and it is either crickets, or it is a wave of attention that teaches you nothing about how to grow predictably. The problem is not the product. It is the shape of the plan.

The Linear Launch: Why It Fails

The linear model is a sequence. Get an idea. Code it. Show a few friends. Decide it is ready. Launch. Collect money. It is completely logical, which is exactly why so many founders run it, and it fails for three reasons.

First, the launch date keeps sliding, because every friend you show it to says some version of "this is cool, but if you added one more thing I would definitely buy." So you go back to coding. That is the one more feature trap. Second, you are asking your network for opinions, not asking them to buy, and nobody wants to be unkind to your face. Third, you put up a website whose messaging you know you will rewrite, because you have not actually settled who your target market is.

Plotted on a chart, a linear launch is a spike, a drop, and then a flat line. There are only two ways it lands:

The good case

You get featured, or you sell a lifetime deal, and a crowd of users floods in. Many are not your ICP. They bought a cheap tool, they complain about it, and now you are stuck supporting them forever.

The worst case

Nothing happens. Nobody pays attention. And you still have no idea whether you just spent months building a product the market does not want.

The Multi-Threaded Launch: The Right Way

A multi-threaded launch runs two threads at the same time, starting at the first commit if not earlier. Thread one is coding and building. Thread two is go-to-market: the early sales and marketing motions that build an audience and get you into real conversations with the people you are building for.

You build a little, then go talk about what you are building. You pull people in, you ask for feedback, and that feedback loops straight back into the product. It tells you whether you are solving the right problem before you have spent a year on the wrong one. Repeat that loop and you do not get one launch, you get a series of mini launches, each one a little bigger than the last.

Nobody remembers the day ChatGPT launched. It accumulated a following quietly and then went vertical, and now every model release is its own launch. That is what a position of power looks like. You can still throw the big launch later, and when you do, you will arrive with customers, proof points, and certainty about who you are talking to.

The 3 Principles of a Launch GTM Plan

  • 1. Idea Stage: Go from zero to one. Nail your Who, the urgent problem, and your value proposition. Sell to your own network.
  • 2. Consistency: Find the pattern. Build your ICP, your Manifesto, and your first Broadway Show.
  • 3. Scale: Run the dual flank strategy. Capture existing demand and create new demand.

Principle 1: The Idea Stage (Zero to One)

At the idea stage your entire go-to-market job is to lay out your core GTM thesis. Three things, and no more:

1

The Who

Who specifically are you building this for? Often you are building for yourself, which is fine, as long as you confirm there are other people like you who share the same insight.

2

The Urgent and Important Problem

Not a nice-to-have. The problem has to be urgent enough that someone will act on it now, and important enough that they will pay to make it go away.

3

The Value Proposition

One sentence that states the problem you solve, the solution you offer, and why it is ten times better than the alternative. This is the thing you actually pitch.

Then you take all three to your existing network, the people who already trust you. Trust is the requirement, not a nicety. Without it you will not get honest feedback, and a stranger at this stage has no reason to engage with a product that does not exist yet.

People will still be nice to you. Some will say it is a brilliant idea, some will say it is terrible, and neither tells you anything. So ask for money. "If we solved it this way, would you pay $100 to use it and be an early tester?" That question separates genuine interest from politeness in about four seconds.

The benchmark is ten customers. It does not matter whether you are selling a $100,000 enterprise platform or a $9 per month micro SaaS tool. Ten people who already trust you, who agree the problem is urgent, and who pay. If you cannot get to ten, go back and reset the thesis. Do not move on to Principle 2. This is precisely where the linear model goes wrong, and founders have spent millions and burned years building the wrong thing because they skipped it.

Key Takeaway

"If you do not have at least ten people you can pitch in your own network, chances are you are disconnected from the problem you are solving. Either make friends fast and build the trust first, or stop and pick a different market."

TK Kader

Two founders in TK's accelerator batch announced they were going to disrupt the wedding industry. Neither had worked in it. Neither had ever been married. They failed, and the reason was not execution. They were disconnected from the Who, so they never understood the problem they claimed to solve.

Once your network is exhausted, then you can go wider. Post on LinkedIn or X about the urgent problem and who you serve, share your value proposition, and ask people to get on a call so you can understand their needs. Nothing fancy. But do that after the network, not instead of it.

Principle 2: Consistency (Where the Money Starts Flowing)

You have ten customers. Now you need ten more, and then ten more after that. The name of the game is no longer proof, it is consistency.

Here is the catch. To land those first ten you probably did eighty different things and said eighty different things. Somewhere in there is a pattern. Finding it is the work of this stage, and it breaks into three pieces:

1

Your Ideal Customer Profile

Look at the customers you have and find the pattern in who gets the most value. A segment, a company type, a title, a trigger. TK runs founders through a 29-point process to flesh this out, because it is what tells you who is buying and who is not, so you can go find more of the former. Start with the free ICP template and the 3-Part Framework above.

2

Your Messaging Manifesto

The value proposition is one sentence. The Manifesto is everything that grows out of it: what you do, the transformation you bring to the market, who the competition is, why you are ten times better, and how you shorten the distance between understanding you and buying from you. Get this right and you stop rewriting your homepage every month. It also settles your cold email, your LinkedIn posts, your lead magnet, your pitch deck, and your ADs.

3

Your First Broadway Show

A Broadway Show is a consistent, repeatable set of sales and marketing activities that puts your message in front of your ICP week after week. This is what converts the flat line into a curve that climbs, because the same message reaches more of the right people every single week.

Notice what makes this possible. A linear launch leaves you with a spike and no data. A multi-threaded launch leaves you with ten customers, a record of what you said to each of them, and who responded. That is the raw material an actual go-to-market strategy is built from, and it is the reason your ICP at this stage is grounded in evidence rather than assumption.

Principle 3: Scale (The Dual Flank)

Consistency earns you the right to scale. You know who you are selling to and exactly what to say, and when your ICP reads your Manifesto they nod. Now you can press on the gas, whether that is raising a round on the strength of a repeatable motion or feeding profits back into the machine.

Here is the shape of the market you are pressing into:

3%
Ready to buy now

Actively researching a solution. You capture this demand by targeting them directly with ADs, now that your messaging and targeting are proven.

97%
Not in market yet

They do not know about you, or do not yet know they have the problem. You create this demand by educating them until they become buyers.

Running at both at once is the dual flank strategy. One Broadway Show captures the demand that already exists in the 3%. A second, larger one creates demand across the rest of the market through ADs, organic content, and events, teaching people they have a problem and why you are ten times better at solving it. Capturing demand and creating demand, running together. That is when it stops being a launch and starts being a machine.

Common Questions

What is the difference between ICP and Buyer Persona?
An Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) defines the target company (firmographics, size, industry), while a Buyer Persona defines the individual people within that company.
Where can I find a free ideal customer profile template?
We provide a free ICP template in this guide that covers the 3-part framework: Firmographics (the "Who"), Triggers (the "When"), and Macro Trends (the "Why Now"). Once you have your ICP, the Launch Program is where TK works with you to turn it into positioning, messaging, and revenue.
Why is an ICP important for SaaS?
SaaS companies with a defined ICP have a 68% higher win rate. It allows you to focus sales and marketing resources on the segment most likely to buy.
How often should I update my ICP?
Your ICP is a living mandate. You should review it quarterly based on Closed-Won/Lost data.
Can a startup have multiple ICPs?
In the early stages (Pre-$10M ARR), focus on ONE core ICP to build momentum. Splitting focus too early dilutes your marketing and slows down product-market fit.
What if my niche is too small?
It is better to dominate a small "Beachhead" market where you are 10x better than to be a commodity in a large market. Once you dominate the niche, you can expand.
How do I validate my ICP?
Validate with currency. Are they willing to pay? Sales velocity is the ultimate validation metric.
How does AI impact ICP development?
AI allows you to enrich your firmographic data instantly. Tools can now track buying signals (hiring, tech stack changes) in real-time, allowing you to target your ICP with precision.

TAM (Total Addressable Market)
The total market demand for a product or service. Often confused with ICP.
SAM (Serviceable Available Market)
The segment of the TAM targeted by your products and services which is within your geographical reach.
SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market)
The portion of SAM that you can capture. This is your immediate ICP focus.
Economic Buyer
The person within the ICP who controls the budget and has the final signing authority.
Champion
The internal stakeholder who actively sells your product on your behalf to the Economic Buyer.
PMF (Product-Market Fit)
When you have identified an ICP that is underserved and have a product that satisfies their strong market demand.
